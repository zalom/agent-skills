# Research

Read when comparing wording with AI-writing research or making frequency claims.

## Separate the kinds of evidence

| Claim | Evidence needed |
|---|---|
| A phrase is more frequent in AI text | A comparison with human text that measures that phrase or clearly defined pattern. |
| A passage repeats a pattern | The supplied text; quote the occurrences or describe their locations. |
| A sentence sounds generic | An editorial explanation of what it fails to communicate, labeled as judgment. |
| A person used AI | Provenance beyond a short passage's style. Do not infer it from these checks. |

Search when research is requested. Prefer primary comparative studies and
state the genre, language, and language model studied when they limit the
finding. A list of phrases observed in generated essays alone does not prove
they are more frequent than in human writing. Academic findings do not provide
application-specific probabilities.

## Starting sources

Use these as research starting points, not a timeless blacklist. Open and
verify a source before presenting a claim from it. Follow newer evidence when
it changes the conclusion.

- [Juzek and Ward, 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.426/):
  lexical overrepresentation in scientific writing, including delve,
  intricate, and underscore. Preserve the scientific-writing scope.
- [Geng and Trotta, 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.657/):
  changes in previously prominent vocabulary markers after public attention.
  Absence of familiar markers cannot establish human authorship.
- [Pew Research Center, August 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2026/08/20/how-much-of-the-internet-is-written-with-ai/):
  recurring vocabulary, punctuation, and rhetorical patterns on webpages.
  Individual documents can be misclassified.

Do not expand a finding about one word into a claim about every synonym, or a
finding about lists into a measured claim about a particular application
phrase. State when no comparative evidence for an exact phrase was found.

## Compare with the draft

Report only features actually present. For mechanical vocabulary checks,
declare the word list and matching method. Distinguish exact matches from
inflections, substrings, and semantic similarity. A zero-match result describes
that list only; it is not a human-authorship verdict.

Keep ordinary grammar out of a blacklist. Repeated triplets can flatten a
draft's rhythm, but a useful triplet does not need repair. Tie each suggested
edit to its meaning or readability, not an attempt to fool a detector.

If browsing is unavailable, identify the missing verification. Continue with
the supplied text and sources, labeling stylistic impressions accurately.
